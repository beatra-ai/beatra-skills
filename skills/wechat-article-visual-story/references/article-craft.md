# Writing the article

The article is read on a phone, in a feed, by someone who arrived from a title and a thumbnail. Everything here follows from that.

## Title candidates

Three, meaningfully different in approach rather than three phrasings of one idea.

| Approach | The move |
| --- | --- |
| The specific claim | A concrete, checkable statement the article then proves |
| The reader's question | The thing they would type, asked back to them |
| The tension | Two things that should not both be true |

Keep each under about twenty-four characters or eight words. Feed listings truncate, and a title whose point lands after the cut has no point.

Front-load the distinguishing word. The reader is scanning a column of titles that all begin the same way; whatever makes this one different should be in the first few characters, not after a set-up clause.

Mark the recommended one and say why in a single line. Do not rank all three with an essay — the user decides in seconds.

## The digest line

One line, about fifty to a hundred and twenty characters, that appears beneath the title in shares and previews.

It is not a summary. It is the second hook: the title earned the glance, this earns the tap. Say the thing the title implied but did not deliver.

## Body structure

Five parts. The proportions matter more than the labels.

| Part | Share | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open | 10 percent | Land in the reader's situation. No throat-clearing, no history of the industry |
| Turn | 10 percent | Name what is actually wrong, or what they have got backwards |
| Substance | 55 percent | The real content, in two to four sections with their own subheads |
| Bring it home | 15 percent | Connect the substance to what is being promoted, honestly |
| Ask | 10 percent | One action |

The open is where most articles are lost. A first paragraph that explains what the article will cover is a first paragraph the reader leaves during. Start inside the situation instead.

## Write for the phone

- **Short paragraphs.** Two or three sentences. A paragraph longer than a phone screen reads as a wall and gets scrolled past rather than read.
- **Subheads that carry meaning.** A reader scanning subheads alone should get the argument. "Part Two" carries nothing.
- **One idea per paragraph.** The second idea in a paragraph is the one nobody remembers.
- **Line breaks as rhythm.** A one-line paragraph after a dense one is emphasis, and it is free.
- **No written-register connectives.** Furthermore, thus, in summary — nobody reads these on a phone, they skim past them.

## Bring it home without the swerve

The most common failure in a promotional article is the paragraph where the tone changes and the reader realises they have been reading an advertisement.

Avoid it by connecting the substance to the promoted thing through what the article has already established, not through a transition sentence. If the article spent 55 percent of its length on a real problem, the product enters as the answer to that specific problem, in the same voice, with the same level of detail. If it cannot enter that way, the substance section was about the wrong thing.

## Screen the copy in the same pass

**Superlatives and absolutes.** Words claiming top rank, totality, or permanence — best, first, number one, absolute, permanent, national-grade, 100% — are restricted in advertising copy in several markets, and their local-language equivalents carry the same restriction. This applies to the title and the digest as much as the body, and titles are where they concentrate.

**Efficacy claims.** Health, medical, curative, weight, whitening, and safety outcomes need substantiation the user actually holds. Long-form invites more claiming than a short post does, so this is where a promotional article most often overreaches.

**Prices.** Every price appears with its unit, size, or condition.

**Comparisons.** A named competitor comparison needs evidence the user holds.

Report what was changed alongside the draft, so the user can reject any of it.

## What the user has to supply

Facts about the promoted thing are theirs. When one is missing, write the paragraph around it — a section can carry a mechanism, a scenario, or a principle without a number in it.

When a missing fact would gut the article's central argument, ask for that one fact rather than writing around it. One question is cheaper than an article that cannot be published.
