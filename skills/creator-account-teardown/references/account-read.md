# Reading the account

An account teardown that has no platform data behind it is not a weaker teardown. It is a different one: it reasons from what a person can actually see on a profile, which is exactly what the user can verify and act on. The discipline that makes it trustworthy is keeping the evidence and the inference visibly apart.

That discipline is unchanged when the account was looked up instead of pasted. A lookup adds a second legitimate source; it does not add a third, and it does not license a cell to be filled from anywhere else. See [reading the account from a handle](account-lookup.md) for the route and its prices.

## The evidence table

Build it first, before any read. Nothing later may reason from anything that is not in it.

| Field | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- |
| Handle, display name, bio, link | The profile, verbatim — looked up or as supplied |
| Follower count, total posts | Only if a lookup returned it or the user supplied it |
| Post title or caption | Verbatim — looked up or as supplied |
| Views, likes, comments, saves, shares | Only the numbers a lookup returned or that are visible in what the user supplied |
| Post date or ordinal position | Looked up, as supplied, or "position N in the list" |
| Format | Read from the post itself: talking head, voiceover over B-roll, text-on-screen, skit, tutorial, montage |

Two rules govern it. Every cell either has a source or is marked unstated, and a looked-up cell also carries the time it was read. And a cell that is unstated stays unstated — it is never filled with a typical value, a ratio derived from another account, or a number that "must be about right". A lookup that came back empty leaves the cell unstated exactly as a missing screenshot does.

Say what the sample is: how many posts, over what span, how they were selected, and whether they were looked up or supplied. A read of the twelve most recent posts is a read of the twelve most recent posts, not a read of the account's history — and one page of a lookup's results is one page, not the account's whole output.

## Positioning

What does this account promise, to whom, and in one line? Derive it from three places and say which one carried it: the bio, the recurring subject across the post list, and the format the account uses most.

Write the positioning as the account itself would state it, then write the sharper version the evidence actually supports. The distance between those two is often the account's real weak point.

## Audience read

Who is this actually for? Build it from what the copy assumes rather than from demographics you cannot see: the vocabulary level, the problems taken as given, the products and prices referenced, the objections handled, and the moment in the day the content fits.

Name the audience as a person with a situation, not a bracket. "Someone six months into a first management role who is being asked to run performance reviews for the first time" is a read. "Ages 25 to 34, urban" is a guess dressed as data.

Where comment text is available — looked up on TikTok, Douyin, or Xiaohongshu, or supplied by the user — say what the comments reveal, and treat it as the strongest direct evidence for who the account actually speaks to rather than who its bio claims. Where there is none, say that the audience read is drawn from the copy alone. Comments are not available for the other platforms on the lookup route, so on those the audience read stays a read of the copy however the account was obtained.

## Content matrix

Sort the posts — looked up or supplied — into pillars — the three to six recurring subjects the account actually publishes. For each pillar record how many posts fall in it, which formats it uses, and the range of visible engagement inside it.

Then read the matrix for its shape:

- **Which pillar carries the account.** The one with the highest visible engagement, stated with the numbers and the sample size behind it.
- **Which pillar exists but does not perform.** Often the one the creator likes most.
- **Which format repeats.** Accounts that scale usually run two or three formats, not eight.
- **What is missing.** A pillar the positioning implies but the grid does not contain is an opening.

State sample sizes inline. A pillar represented by two posts is a signal, not a finding, and should be labelled that way.

## The pattern behind the top performers

Take the posts with the highest visible engagement and read them against each other, not in isolation.

For each, record the opening line or opening frame verbatim, the structure it follows, the length, and the ending move. Then look for what repeats across them:

- **The hook move.** Question, contradiction, number, warning, direct address, before-and-after, or a claim that sounds wrong. Name it, and quote the lines that share it.
- **The structure.** Problem then answer, list, story then lesson, demonstration, teardown, comparison.
- **The ending.** What it asks for, or that it asks for nothing.
- **What is constant across pillars.** The move that survives a subject change is the one that transfers.

Everything in this section is inference. Write it as inference: the pattern, the posts that support it, how many posts that is, and what would confirm it — usually the same structure repeating across a wider sample, or the same structure failing to appear in the weakest posts.

Do not attribute performance to anything not in the evidence table. Timing, promotion, platform push, and off-platform traffic are all plausible causes and none of them are visible here. Say so once, plainly, and move on.

## Cadence

From the dates or positions supplied, read how often the account publishes and whether the rhythm is steady or bursty. When dates are not supplied, say cadence is unstated rather than inferring it from post count.

Where cadence is visible, note whether the strong posts cluster — a run of strong posts in one week reads differently from strong posts spread across a quarter.

## The account's weak point

Every account has one, and finding it is what turns a teardown into an opening. Look for a pillar that underperforms its share of the grid, a positioning line the content does not deliver, a format the audience clearly wants more of, an audience question the comments keep asking that the content never answers, or a cadence that broke.

State it with the evidence and the sample size. This is where the user's own account gets to win.

## The gap read

Put the analysed account beside the user's own. Four questions:

1. **What transfers.** Structure, format, cadence, hook move — the parts that do not depend on being that specific creator.
2. **What does not.** Anything resting on their face, their access, their existing audience, their location, or their production budget.
3. **What the user has that they do not.** Expertise, proof, access, a product, a point of view, a niche within the niche.
4. **Where the opening is.** Usually the analysed account's weak point crossed with the user's advantage.

When the user has no account yet, questions 1, 2, and 4 still run; question 3 comes from what they told you about themselves.
